Tuna Piece
A 2D motion graphic interpreting Yoko Ono’s “Tunafish Sandwich Piece” through transience, wabi-sabi, and the quiet return from grandeur to everyday life.
Index




Type
      2D motion graphic, illustration, experimental animation

Year
      2025

Reference
      “Tunafish Sandwich Piece” by Yoko Ono, 1964

Role
      Concept, 2D illustration, motion design, character rigging, compositing

Tools / Methods
      Photoshop, Illustrator, After Effects, DUIK rigging, Risograph color palette, watercolor texture, canvas paper texture, noise, Turbulent Displacement

View Process Book Overview

    Tuna Piece is a 2D motion graphic developed from Yoko Ono’s 1964 instruction piece, “Tunafish Sandwich Piece.” The work interprets the poem’s contrast between cosmic scale and ordinary action: a sky filled with one thousand suns, followed by the simple act of making and eating a tuna sandwich.

    The animation translates this contrast into a movement between two emotional states. The first is grandeur: the extraordinary, magnificent, and short-lived moment. The second is simplicity: the quiet return to everyday life, intimacy, stillness, and doing nothing.

    Rather than treating the poem as a literal sequence, the project uses it as a structure for thinking about transience, imperfection, and the beauty of ordinary daily experience.  





Design Question     How can a short instruction piece be translated into a visual movement from extraordinary grandeur to mundane simplicity?  

Concept     The poem presents a juxtaposition between grandiose imagery and mundane simplicity. The image of one thousand suns creates a sense of wonder and magnificence, but the poem gives this vision a short duration: the suns shine for only one hour before gradually melting into the sky.

    This temporary grandeur is then interrupted by an ordinary human action: making and eating a tuna sandwich. The shift brings the viewer back to daily life and suggests an acceptance of transience, imperfection, and simplicity.

    I connected this attitude to wabi-sabi: the beauty of what is imperfect, impermanent, and incomplete. In the animation, grandeur appears through the performing stage, lights, flowers, and dance, while simplicity appears through the small domestic space, lying on the floor, listening to music, and doing nothing.  


Visual References     The visual direction began with Ukiyo-e paintings. I referenced their depictions of daily life, pleasures, minimal brushwork, and use of negative space as a way to connect the project to a quiet wabi-sabi sensibility.

    I also studied Seiichi Hayashi’s illustrations as a modern continuation of this minimal visual language. His work helped me develop a simpler and more restrained illustration approach.

    Océane Muller’s work informed the more dreamlike side of the animation, especially the use of gradients, textures, brushstrokes, and poetic atmosphere.  


Storyboard     The animation is structured in four scenes:

Scene 1 — Home
    A girl lies at home doing nothing. As she closes her eyes, she begins to sink into her thoughts.

Scene 2 — Performing Stage
    The scene match-cuts to a performing stage, where the girl dances surrounded by hundreds of stage lights. A close-up creates the transition into the next scene.

Scene 3 — Flower Field
    At the peak of her emotion, she dances in a flower field under thousands of lights.

Scene 4 — Home
    A burning light flare transitions the animation back to her home: the reality of ordinary daily life.  


Art Direction     After testing different visual directions, I decided to use a Risograph-inspired color palette. This palette helped the animation feel soft, imperfect, and printed, rather than overly clean or digital.

    The final art direction combines delicate linework, muted color, gradients, and paper-like texture. Watercolor, canvas paper, and noise textures were added in After Effects to create a tactile surface. Turbulent Displacement was used to give the animation a subtle hand-drawn quality.  


Motion Process     The dancing movements were planned through storyboard frames and motion references. I referenced ballet movement, especially from the film Black Swan, to develop the dancing sequences.

    After sketching the frames in Photoshop, I finalized the character design for the performance scenes. The character was illustrated in Illustrator, separated into body parts, and rigged with the DUIK plugin for animation in After Effects.

    During the process, some Illustrator textures and stroke styles could not be transferred properly into After Effects. I revised the Illustrator files, converted layers into shape layers, rebuilt textures directly in After Effects, and adjusted shape paths, camera position, object perspective, and keyframes throughout the sequence.  


Technical Development     The project required a detailed workflow between Illustrator and After Effects. After importing AI files into AE, I had to rearrange the converted shape layers and rebuild visual details that were not preserved during the transfer.

    Much of the production focused on adjusting character features, controlling 3D camera movement, arranging object perspective, and refining keyframes. Working with 3D perspective in After Effects became one of the most challenging and useful parts of the project.  


Outcome     The final outcome is a 2D motion graphic that moves from a small domestic scene into an imagined performance space, then returns to everyday life.

    The animation uses dance, light, flowers, texture, and stillness to interpret the poem’s movement from magnificence to simplicity. The piece does not present grandeur and ordinary life as opposites. Instead, it shows how the extraordinary can dissolve back into the quietness of daily experience.  


Reflection     Tuna Piece helped me understand motion design as a way of translating an abstract text into rhythm, atmosphere, and visual transition.

    Through this project, I became interested in how animation can hold both spectacle and stillness. The work begins with an imagined grandeur, but its emotional center lies in the return to something simple: a quiet room, a body at rest, and the acceptance that even magnificent moments are temporary.
