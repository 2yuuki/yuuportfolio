The Memory Index
A web-based reflective tool translating personal digital memories into a collective ASCII artwork on traditional student notebook and Do paper.

Featured in Thanh Nien News The Insight

    The Memory Index began during a period when I felt emotionally numb and disconnected from my own memories. I noticed that we often tell ourselves to ignore strong emotions so we can keep moving, but from my own experience, respecting what we feel is part of how we understand ourselves and grow.

    I wanted to make a gentle tool that asks people to slow down, write, look back, and leave a visual trace of what they remember. Its visual language comes from things that feel familiar to me: Vietnamese student notebooks, purple ink, study outlines, administrative documents, Zing Me, and the early internet. I combined these references with net.art and ASCII because both can make something personal feel like a system that can be read, revisited, and shared.  


How It Works     The project changed many times before it became this tool. I first imagined an animated film about forgotten memories, then a 3D childhood character that people could draw onto, and later a printable paper fortune-teller. Each version taught me something, but none of them gave people enough space to reflect.

    I eventually returned to the most familiar method: journaling. From there, the final experience became four simple actions:

1. Write
    Users input journaling reflections to anchor their feelings in words.

2. Process
    Personal photos are algorithmically converted into computational ASCII textures with a nostalgic, marker-like threshold effect.

3. Sketch
    Replacing freehand mouse drawing, a custom ASCII painting tool lets users compose images by placing characters into a grid—making the act of drawing as deliberate and relaxing as playing with LEGO.

4. Manifest
    Individual digital diagrams are printed on physical student notebook paper or Do paper and physically sewn together to form a large, collective exhibition artwork.  





Process & Technical Development     I built the two main tools in p5.js: an image processor and an ASCII painter. The first freehand drawing prototype felt awkward with a mouse, so I changed it into a grid where people select a character and place it like LEGO. My friends described the process as relaxing, which confirmed that the simpler interaction worked better.

    Their feedback also led me to add local storage and limit the image processor to 24 FPS so the website could run more reliably. Visually, I moved away from a HyperCard-inspired interface because it felt too distant from Vietnamese nostalgia, and returned to the language of study sheets, notebooks, sticky tabs, and familiar printed documents.  



Project showcased at IFFTI 2026 Conference.


Exhibition & Outcome     After the website became functional, I collected drawings and images made by my friends and printed them on Vietnamese Do paper. Because I could only print at A4 size at home, I needed a way to connect many sheets without using tape or staples. Sewing felt right: it reminded me of the small embroidery kits we used in primary school, and allowed the separate memories to physically hold onto each other.

    At the exhibition, I first expected the website to be the main focus. After testing the setup, I realized people understood the project more quickly when they encountered the printed Memory Diagrams first. I reorganized the booth like a small gallery, then invited visitors to use the website and make their own results.

    Seeing people stop, look, and spend time with the work gave me confidence to continue making projects where graphic design, interaction, and emotion meet.

    Recognition: Showcased at IFFTI 2026 and featured by Thanh Nien News.
