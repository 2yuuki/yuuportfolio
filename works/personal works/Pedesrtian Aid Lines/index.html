Pedestrian Aid Lines
A cooperative road-safety game that translates pedestrian vulnerability into communication, navigation, and map-based decision-making.

Play Game
Desktop recommended Overview

    Pedestrian Aid Lines began with a simple role-playing idea: what if a child could become a phone operator whose job is to help pedestrians? One player works at the PAL headquarters while another moves through the city. They have to talk, read the map together, and guide people to the place they need.  


Process
Open Game Design Document in a new tab



Research Question     How can a cooperative game help young players understand pedestrian safety, accessibility, and the diverse mobility needs of people in urban environments?  

Context     Road safety is often taught through rules and warnings. I wanted the learning to happen through cooperation and care instead. The pedestrian may be in a hurry, confused, vulnerable, or facing a mobility challenge, so the operator has to listen before deciding how to help.  


Game System     The game is built around three actions: understand the pedestrian’s request, find a suitable destination, and guide them along a safe route. The manual includes the city map, location keys, hints, and stickers so players can solve each situation together rather than follow one fixed answer.  


Design Approach     As game designer and art director, I worked on the world, characters, environment logic, and the connection between gameplay and road-safety learning. We designed the city to feel green, modern, and friendly, with familiar destinations such as the hospital, school, market, park, and community centre alongside bicycle sharing, electric stations, and solar bus stops.  



Game design document excerpts: gameplay rules, city logic, character needs, map system, and visual development.

Outcome     The final game turns safety into a shared responsibility between two players. The map is not only an interface; it helps children notice how infrastructure, accessibility, and everyday decisions affect different pedestrians.  





Reflection     This project showed me that a serious subject does not always need a serious-looking solution. Through character stories, playful navigation, and conversation, the game lets children practise attention, responsibility, and care for other people in public space.
